01 · THE STORY

Start with the patient's concern

Think about the first thing the patient would say if they were explaining their problem to a friend.

Keep this short. Use normal everyday language.

  • “I thought I was just tired.”
  • “My headaches were becoming part of my day.”
  • “My stomach has not felt the same for a while.”
  • “I kept feeling dizzy and didn't know why.”
  • “I wasn't sleeping properly anymore.”

This just helps suggest relevant conversation questions in the next step — it isn't shown to patients as a diagnosis.

A short phrase shown as a tag under the title, e.g. “On headaches that keep coming back.”

Briefly explain why the patient came to you. Do not reveal identifying information. Keep this to 1–3 sentences — you don't need to explain the diagnosis or treatment here.

02 · THE PATIENT'S WORDS

Tell it as a conversation

This section is a simple conversation between you and the patient. Ask questions that help another person understand what the experience actually felt like. Keep the answers as close as possible to the patient's own words — they don't need to sound professional.

Suggested questions for this topic — click to add one (optional, not mandatory):
Ask what helps someone understand the experience. "What were you most worried about?" · "What was the hardest part of dealing with this?" · "Did it affect your sleep?" · "Did it change anything about your daily routine?" · "Did you initially think it was something else?" · "Was there something you were avoiding because of it?"

Try to avoid questions that sound like an examination or medical questionnaire — and don't ask for full names, addresses, phone numbers, workplace, exact age, or other identifying details.
03 · YOU MIGHT RELATE TO THIS

Help another patient recognise themselves

Think about what someone reading this story might recognise from their own life. These should describe experiences, not diagnoses — avoid conclusions like "this proves you have…" or "you may have…".

Add 2–5 short points describing what a patient might have noticed, e.g. “You have been feeling tired for several weeks.”

04 · WHAT YOU CAN DO

Give a simple next step

This should be general guidance, not a personalised diagnosis or treatment plan. Keep it practical and easy to understand — avoid making promises about outcomes.
